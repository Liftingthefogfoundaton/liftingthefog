Mission Statement

Foreword

What is Lifting the Fog?

Lifting the Fog: Fire of God is a 12-Action spiritual program that was created through prayerful reflection, lived experience and intentional writing. It exists to invite individuals and communities into clarity, healing and deeper relationships with God and one another. We believe that freedom comes through connection and that transformation is sustained through honesty, shared responsibility, and daily spiritual practice.

All are welcome. Participation is open to people of every background, belief system, faith tradition, or non-religious perspective. No prior affiliation is required, and no one is excluded based on where they come from or what they believe. Each person is free to engage at their own pace and according to their own understanding.

In this book, the acronym FOG stands for Fire of God. When the weather is foggy, you can’t see clearly. The Son burns off the fog, and it is lifted. Clarity is restored.

The Twelve Actions and Twelve Traditions presented here offer a God-centered guide for spiritual growth, honesty, and shared community.

We believe personal privacy, dignity, and safety are essential to a healthy community. What is shared within our spaces is treated with care and respect. We prioritize the safety of all participants and recognize that involving appropriate local authorities may be necessary when safety is at risk.

Our mission is to support individuals and groups as they move from isolation toward connection, from self-direction toward discernment, and from harm toward repair.

We value local stewardship, voluntary participation, and spiritual independence, trusting that growth happens best when responsibility is shared and service is offered freely.

We seek not to fix, control, or direct others, but to walk alongside them—listening, learning, and living these principles together—so that what has been given to us may be carried outward with wisdom, compassion, and care. May this work serve as an invitation to walk this journey of faith and “come home” to a relationship with God and one another.

Opening story

My name is Jason. I am a child of God who struggles with sin, but through God’s grace and mercy I get to be here and share this story with you.

According to the world, I have a Grade 5 education. This means that academic skills, such as reading and writing, were never really developed. As a child, I was homeschooled and grew up in a household out in the woods without proper structure or education, so I never really learned how to read or write.

The fact that I can do what I have done today is truly a miracle. Because of the technology we have now, I am able to speak into a computer, and with the help of programs designed to assist people like me, my words can be written down. What once felt impossible, has slowly become possible.

I have been a member of Alcoholics Anonymous for 29 years. I was sober for 20 of those years before I felt confident enough to read the 9th Step Promises in an AA meeting, and even that took serious courage.

Growing up in the Yukon, our home was often filled with strangers. There was very little supervision, and as a result, many unhealthy things happened. Those experiences shaped me in ways I did not fully understand at the time.

Through the program of Alcoholics Anonymous, I learned the process of recovery. This does not mean I have all the answers—it means I have understanding. Sometimes, understanding is enough to keep moving forward.

After working the Steps, I had an encounter with God that changed everything for me. I was trying to help a young man come into AA, and together we made an agreement that if he came to an AA meeting with me, I would go to church with him. Interestingly, he never came to AA with me, but I did go to the church he suggested.

During that time, I was having an honest conversation with God, asking Him to explain Himself to me, so that I could better understand who He truly was. Up to this point, I was unsure. One day at church, I felt compelled to go forward to the front where people were worshiping.

In that moment, I felt a presence come over me. It felt as though everything I had ever done in my life—every burden and every weight—was lifted and removed. I also began speaking in a language I had never heard before and did not understand. At that time, I had never read the Bible, so I had no framework for what was happening.

After that experience, I went home and prayed quietly. I said, “God, if You want me to get to know You, you’re going to have to get me a book.” I never told anyone about that prayer.

Two days later, two people showed up at my house with Bibles. At that point, I had never read the Bible and could not read it. So, I asked God to teach me how to read.

The truth is, I have never read another book on my own. And yet, here I am—not because of ability, not because of education, but because of God’s grace.

During the years that followed, my wife and I had many conversations about what it might look like if there was a program for everyday people who may not struggle with addiction, but who still struggle with life, relationships, and purpose. During this time, I spent two and a half years on my knees, seeking God and trying to understand.

At times, I became what some might call overly religious. I couldn’t understand why others couldn’t do what I was doing, and I struggled with frustration and judgment. Looking back, I can see that God was shaping my heart, not giving me a position.

During that season, I felt inspired to create something similar to Alcoholics Anonymous—a place for people who may not struggle with alcoholism, but who still struggle with selfishness, fear, isolation, and broken relationships.

Alcoholics Anonymous taught me something sacred: it does not matter how many times you relapse, what you have done, or where you have been—you are loved. That love is unconditional. Every alcoholic who stays sober long enough comes to understand that without God’s help and without helping another person, old behaviors return.

I have seen thousands of alcoholics stop going to meetings, stop helping others, and stop doing the next right thing. Every single one of them eventually went back out until they found a new rock bottom.

My heart longed for the world to experience the gift that Alcoholics Anonymous had given to me.

These Twelve Actions are designed for every person to walk through healing—one action at a time—with God’s help, by learning to love ourselves and others.

The truth is none of us have all of the answers. None of us see God fully, yet in His grace and mercy, we are invited into relationship with Him: The God who created the heavens and the earth.

Through practicing these Actions, attending regular meetings, serving our communities, and learning to love our neighbors, we can experience a life filled with the gifts that God has to offer.

One beautiful thing about Lifting the Fog meetings is that people come alive. Masks fall away. Isolation loosens its grip. Whether in a small group or a larger one, we begin to realize that we are far more alike than different.

In these spaces, God can carry a message through attentive listening and honest sharing. What is shared remains safe within the group. We listen without fixing, advising, or controlling. We allow God to do what He does best—change the heart, restore the mind, and transform the environment we live in.

As you enter this journey, my prayer is simple: ask God for the willingness to see what you need to see, to hear what you need to hear, and to allow your heart to remain open to what God wants to transform in you.

Not my will, but Yours be done.

Action One

Powerlessness and Surrender

We recognize the limits of our own understanding and open our lives to God’s wisdom.

The Spiritual Movement of Action One

Action One begins with honesty. Not dramatic honesty, not despairing honesty, but clear-eyed truthfulness about what it means to be human. Each of us carries gifts, intelligence, effort, and intention. Yet there are places where even our best efforts do not bring peace, connection, or lasting change. Action One invites us to stop pretending otherwise.

This Action is not about weakness; it is about reality. It recognizes that self-reliance has limits. When we accept those limits, something opens. We become teachable. We make room for God’s wisdom rather than insisting that life conform to our preferences. Action One is the doorway through which spiritual life begins, because it shifts us from self-direction to God-awareness.

My Personal Journey

Action One was one of the most difficult realizations of my life. Part of that struggle was my age. How could it be that I was powerless over anything? And how could my life already be unmanageable when it had only just begun? No strong human being wants to believe they are incapable of managing their own circumstances.

For me, it was both frightening and profound when I finally realized that I needed help. Deep down, I knew that if I continued relying solely on myself, I might one day wake up at fifty-six years old, walking the same path my father had walked—avoiding the truth and failing to recognize the patterns I had been participating in all along.

Action One is about accepting a simple but powerful truth: we are not in control. When we rely only on ourselves, our circumstances often create more pain, not less. At the time, many of us cannot see the value in pain. Yet pain is often what brings us to a place where we begin seeking understanding about what is truly happening in our lives.

With God’s help, and through connection and fellowship with others, we begin to see more clearly. The same thinking and self-reliance we once believed would be the solution is often what led us to powerlessness in the first place. What we once trusted as the answer eventually reveals itself as part of the problem.

The good news is that when I came to this understanding, I no longer had to fight the battle alone. Accepting my powerlessness and recognizing that my life had become unmanageable when left to my own devices did not make me weak. In fact, it did the opposite. It empowered me.

I realized that I no longer had to carry the weight of life by myself.

Thank You God, for bringing me to this place. Thank You for allowing me to see the next action I can take, so that I may become the person You have called me to be.

Why this Action Matters

Action One invites us into honesty—not just about what we do, but about the patterns that have quietly shaped our lives over time. Many of the struggles we face are not the result of one poor choice or one bad moment, but of repeated moments: where we spend our time, who we allow influence over us, and what we return to when no one is watching.

This recognition is not meant to produce shame. It is meant to bring clarity. When we begin to see that our behaviors have been shaped by environments, relationships, and rhythms, we are able to tell the truth about what is and is not working.

Admitting the truth requires humility. We acknowledge that while we may desire change, remaining in the same conditions often produces the same results. This does not mean we are weak; it means we are being invited to take responsibility for our lives in a deeper, more honest way.

Action One helps us recognize that behavior follows exposure. What we repeatedly place ourselves around will eventually shape how we think, respond, and live. Old environments often reinforce old reactions. Without awareness, we can unknowingly return to places, people, or habits that keep us stuck.

This Action is not about immediate correction or drastic change. It is about seeing clearly. Before transformation can occur, truth must be acknowledged. We begin by admitting that our current way of living—no matter how familiar—has not led us to the freedom, peace, or connection we desire.

Action One calls us to pause, reflect, and tell the truth about our patterns, trusting that God meets us not in perfection, but in honesty. This admission becomes the foundation upon which all further changes are built.

What Usually Blocks Us Here

Many of us resist Action One because it feels like failure or defeat. We are taught to manage, fix, control, and succeed. Admitting limits can feel like giving up. Some people confuse humility with humiliation, believing that acknowledging limitations means they are unworthy. Action One rejects this lie.

Another common block is partial honesty. We may admit difficulty in theory while continuing to manage outcomes in practice. True Action One living happens in pauses—when we stop reacting and allow God to guide our next action.

How Action One Is Lived

Action One is lived through daily awareness. We notice where anxiety, resentment, fear, or exhaustion arise. Instead of pushing harder, we pause. We ask what is being demanded of us that only God can provide.

Living Action One does not remove responsibility. It reframes it. We remain active participants in our lives, but we are no longer the sole authority. Decisions are approached with prayer, reflection, and counsel. Over time, we learn to recognize when effort becomes strain, and when surrender becomes wisdom.

Practices That Support Action One

  • Daily Pause: Before reacting, take three deep breaths and ask, “God, what is true here?”
  • Reality Inventory: Write two lists— what you can influence and what you cannot. Give the second list to God.
  • Help-Seeking: Choose one trusted person with whom you can be honest when you feel stuck.
  • Body Awareness: Notice where tension lives in the body and let it signal the need for God’s wisdom.

Questions for Reflection

  1. Where does my self-reliance produce anxiety or isolation?
  2. What am I trying to control right now?
  3. What does open to God’s wisdom look like in this situation?
  4. Who can I invite into honest conversation without defensiveness?
  5. What is one small action today that reflects trust rather than control?

A Closing Prayer

God of wisdom, we release the pressure to have all the answers. We acknowledge where our ways are not bringing peace. Make us willing to learn, ready to listen, and open to Your guidance. Lead us in the next right action.

Amen.

How Funds from Book Sales Are Shared

Your purchase stays in your community. Wherever a book is sold, in whatever town or city, the money goes to work in that same place. The food bank and community organization shares support that town or city directly, and the Foundation’s share is used there too, so every community can see how its support for Lifting The Fog Foundation is invested back into the community.

Lifting The Fog Foundation is committed to using book sales to support the continued development of the program while also giving back to the community. Our intention is to help individuals grow closer to God and one another and to support organizations that serve people in need.

These funds are shared after the costs of developing, manufacturing, and shipping each book have been paid.

For each Lifting The Fog book sold, the funds are allocated as follows:

  • 20% to the writer, in recognition of the work involved in creating the books.

The remaining 80% is divided equally among three recipients:

  • Lifting The Fog Foundation, to support the program and its growth in the town or city where the book was sold.
  • A food bank in the town or city where the book was sold, to help provide food to people and families in need.
  • A community organization in the town or city where the book was sold, to support people facing challenges in that community.

This means that each of the three recipients receives one-third of the remaining 80% (26% of the book-sale amount each). The small amount that remains (2%) is used for administration costs.

This arrangement reflects our commitment to service, accountability, and giving back. As Lifting The Fog Foundation grows, our hope is that the support we provide to local communities will grow with it.

Note: This description concerns book sales. Group Seventh Tradition contributions are handled under a separate sharing arrangement.

How Funds from Book Sales Are Shared

Your purchase stays in your community. Wherever a book is sold, in whatever town or city, the money goes to work in that same place. The food bank and community organization shares support that town or city directly, and the Foundation’s share is used there too, so every community can see how its support for Lifting The Fog Foundation is invested back into the community.

Lifting The Fog Foundation is committed to using book sales to support the continued development of the program while also giving back to the community. Our intention is to help individuals grow closer to God and one another and to support organizations that serve people in need.

These funds are shared after the costs of developing, manufacturing, and shipping each book have been paid.

For each Lifting The Fog book sold, the funds are allocated as follows:

  • 20% to the writer, in recognition of the work involved in creating the books.

The remaining 80% is divided equally among three recipients:

  • Lifting The Fog Foundation, to support the program and its growth in the town or city where the book was sold.
  • A food bank in the town or city where the book was sold, to help provide food to people and families in need.
  • A community organization in the town or city where the book was sold, to support people facing challenges in that community.

This means that each of the three recipients receives one-third of the remaining 80% (26% of the book-sale amount each). The small amount that remains (2%) is used for administration costs.

This arrangement reflects our commitment to service, accountability, and giving back. As Lifting The Fog Foundation grows, our hope is that the support we provide to local communities will grow with it.

Note: This description concerns book sales. Group Seventh Tradition contributions are handled under a separate sharing arrangement.

Our housekeeping rules are to prevent us from getting sidetracked, and we don’t want to miss the purpose of our daily growth. We kindly ask that all electronic devices that are not being used for the purpose of the meeting be turned off to prevent distractions.

The Power to Change

Changing our behaviour only happens when the pain of staying the same becomes greater than the pain of changing. In order for us to grow in our relationship with our Father we have to understand that He is not going to do it for us, but instead He will show us His grace and be there to walk alongside us.

It states in the Bible, "So then each of us will give an account of himself to God", as well, we must confess our sins to another, so that we may be healed. This can only happen when we are honest with ourselves about situations and circumstances in our lives.

This group is about transparency with our feelings of where we are at and there is no judgment taking place. We understand that there is going to be relapse in our behaviours, but we are all taking accountability for our actions to have healthier relationships. Our actions define who we are and without change we will fall into the same old behaviours. We are here to help one another in their journey.

God created us so that we could have relationships with Him and each other. The fundamentals of Lifting the Fog are to grow and love one another, as Jesus is walking with us and loving us on this journey.

A Path to Spiritual Growth

No one among us has been able to maintain anything like perfect adherence to these actions. The point is that we are willing to grow along spiritual lines. The teachings set down are guides to a Spiritual Growth. We claim spiritual progress rather than spiritual perfection.

“I tell you the truth, unless a kernel of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains only a single seed. But if it dies, it produces many seeds.” (John 12:24, NIV)

The problem is that we tend to get impatient, so we dig up the seed to check the progress, and this slows down our growth. When you think about your walk with Jesus, some days you may wonder “Why is this so hard? I’m still struggling with so many problems. When will I finally get it right?”

If you’re not seeing as much fruit as you’d like, don’t despair! Growth takes time.

Husband and wife arguing a lot?

You are not alone, and you are not broken. Most couples get stuck in the same fight again and again. There is a way out, and you can start today.

A note before you begin: these are only a few suggestions to encourage change. We are not counsellors, and we do not claim to be professionals. We are simply trying to encourage change, in one way or another.

How to use this page: read it together, or one at a time and then talk. Take one idea, try it for a week, then come back for the next. You don't need to do it all at once.

For her: if you are tired and hurting

I'm so glad you're here. What you feel matters. Maybe you have been carrying more than anyone sees, and you are worn out from trying, from crying, from wondering if anything will change. You are not too much, and you are not wrong to want to be heard and cared for. God sees you. Take a slow breath. You don't have to fix everything today.

Small things that can help

  • Say what you need early and kindly, before you are worn out. "I feel ___, and what I need is ___." Don't wait for him to guess.
  • Your feelings are real, and you don't have to prove them. Say them calmly and slowly, and ask him to simply listen first.
  • Tell him what he does right. Many men give up when they only hear what is wrong. A thank you can soften a heart.
  • Ask for a time-out when you feel flooded. "I want to talk about this, and I need twenty minutes first."
  • Look after yourself. Sleep, eat, take a walk, pray alone, spend time with a safe friend. You can't pour from an empty cup.
  • Don't carry it alone. Talk to a woman you trust, a pastor, or a counsellor. Being heard by someone else helps you to be calm with him.
  • Give him a clear next step. "It would help me if you would ___ this week." Clear and small works better than a long list.

A prayer for her: Lord, you see how tired I am. Hold my heart. Give me peace, strength and the right words. Soften my husband's heart and mine. Help us start again. Amen.

For him: how to help her feel safe

Hear this gently. Often when she is upset, she is not trying to win. She is hoping to feel safe, listened to and chosen. You don't have to fix it. Start by listening.

Small things that can help

  • Listen first, don't fix. Put the phone down, look at her, and say, "I'm here, and I want to understand."
  • Don't say "calm down" or "you're overreacting." Those words make the hurt bigger. Try "That sounds really hard. Tell me more."
  • Stay with her, or say when you will be back. Walking out or going silent feels like being left. Say, "I need twenty minutes, and I will come back."
  • Ask: "What do you need from me right now?" Then do that one thing.
  • Notice and help without being asked. Dishes, the children, a text during the day, a hug. Small kindnesses fill her up.
  • Say sorry quickly and mean it. "I'm sorry for how I said that" can end an argument in a minute. You are not losing by saying it.
  • Pray for her and with her. Even two minutes, holding hands. Ephesians 5:25 asks husbands to love their wives with the kind of love that gives itself.

A prayer for him: Lord, make me a man who listens. Take away my pride and my anger. Show me how to love my wife well and help me to speak gently. Amen.

Why do we keep having the same fight?

Most arguments are not really about the dishes, the money or the phone. They are about something underneath. Here are the most common reasons couples get stuck.

1. We are fighting about the topic, but the real need is hidden

Under "you never help around the house" is often "I feel alone and unseen". Under "you spend too much" is often "I'm afraid and I don't feel safe". Ask yourself: what am I really afraid of, or missing, right now?

2. One chases, the other pulls away

One person pushes to talk it out right now. The other goes quiet or leaves to calm down. The more one chases, the more the other runs, and the more the other runs, the harder the first one chases. Neither is the bad guy. It is a cycle, and the cycle is the enemy.

3. Our bodies get overwhelmed

When the heart is racing and the voice is rising, the thinking part of the brain shuts down. Nothing helpful gets said in that state, and it usually makes things worse. This is why a pause helps so much.

4. Old hurts and old habits come along

How your parents handled anger, old wounds, and past hurts in the marriage can make a small thing feel huge. Sometimes you are not just reacting to today. You are reacting to every time before.

5. We are tired, hungry, stressed or stretched thin

Lack of sleep, money worries, work, children, health and loneliness make everyone shorter-tempered. The arguments get worse when we are worn out.

6. Some problems never fully go away

Many couples have a few differences that last for life: tidy or messy, saver or spender, social or quiet. The goal is not to win. The goal is to talk about it kindly and find a way to live with it.

What to do in the heat of the moment

Stop and breathe. Slow breath in, longer breath out, five times. It calms the body quickly.

Call a time-out. Say: "I want to work this out, and I'm too upset right now. Can we take 20 minutes and come back?" Then really come back.

Lower your voice. A gentle answer turns away anger. Soft doesn't mean weak.

Take a walk or pray alone. Use the break to calm down, not to build your case. Don't replay the fight in your head.

Things to stop doing

  • Name-calling and put-downs. Attack the problem, not the person.
  • Contempt: eye-rolling, sneering, mocking. It does more damage than almost anything else.
  • "You always" and "you never". They make the other person defend instead of listen.
  • Bringing up the past to win the present.
  • Shutting down completely and giving the silent treatment. Take a break, then return.
  • Arguing in front of the children, or by text.
  • Going to bed angry with nothing said. Scripture says not to let the sun go down on your anger. At least say "I love you, and we'll talk tomorrow."

A simple way to talk it out

When you are both calm, try this. One person is the speaker, the other is the listener. Then switch.

  1. Start gently. Use this pattern: "I feel ___ when ___, and what I need is ___." For example: "I feel lonely when we're on our phones all evening, and what I need is twenty minutes together."
  2. Listener repeats it back. "What I hear you saying is ___. Did I get that right?" Don't argue yet. Just understand.
  3. Ask: "Is there more?" until the speaker feels fully heard.
  4. Say what you understand about their side, even if you see it differently. "That makes sense, because ___."
  5. Switch roles.
  6. Pick one small thing each of you will do this week, and write it down.

Be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to become angry. That one verse is a whole plan.

Habits that make fights smaller

  • Five good for every bad. Couples who stay together have far more kind moments than hard ones. Say thank you every day. Notice what your spouse does right.
  • A 10-minute daily check-in. No phones. Ask: "How are you really? What was hard today? What do you need from me?"
  • Repair quickly. After a fight, say "I'm sorry for how I said that" or "I want to start over". The couples who last aren't the ones who never fight. They are the ones who make up.
  • Watch your body. Sleep, eat, move and rest. Many fights happen when one or both are exhausted or hungry.
  • Weekly time together. A walk, coffee, a meal, anything that isn't about problems.
  • Touch and kindness. A hug, a hand on the shoulder, a kind note.
  • Take turns. Sometimes one gives in. Sometimes the other does. Fair over time, not equal every day.

Bring God into the middle of it

  • Pray together, even for two minutes, holding hands. It is hard to stay angry with someone you are praying with. Pray for them, not just for yourself.
  • Take the log out of your own eye first. Jesus asks us to look at our own part before the other person's. Ask: "What is my share in this?"
  • Remember what you promised. Put on compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness and patience, and forgive.
  • Two are better than one. Ecclesiastes 4:9-10. You are on the same team, facing the problem together.
  • Think of the other person's needs before your own, and speak only what builds up.

A short prayer you can use:
Lord, we are tired of hurting each other. Calm our hearts and soften our words. Show each of us our own part. Help us listen, forgive quickly, and love the way You love us. Bring us back together. Amen.

Questions to ask each other

Pick one a day, when things are calm.

  • What do you feel most when we argue? What are you most afraid of?
  • What is one thing I do that makes you feel loved? One thing that hurts?
  • What did you see growing up when your parents were angry?
  • When do we usually fight (time of day, tired, hungry, money)? How can we plan around that?
  • What is one thing I could do this week to help you feel more cared for?
  • What do we want our home to feel like?

When to get more help

Asking for help is a sign of strength. Consider talking to a pastor, a trained marriage counsellor or a Christian counsellor if:

  • the same fights keep happening for months and nothing changes,
  • one or both of you feel hopeless, or are thinking about leaving,
  • there is an affair, addiction, serious debt or a deep betrayal,
  • you can't talk without it blowing up.

The Foundation's recovery meetings and prayer support can walk alongside you, but we are not a replacement for professional counselling.

If you are afraid of your partner. Hitting, threats, forcing, controlling who you see or what you do, or making you afraid is not "arguing". It is abuse, and this page is not the answer. Please get safe first.

  • In an emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in Canada and the USA).
  • British Columbia: VictimLinkBC, 1-800-563-0808, free, 24 hours.
  • USA: National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233.
  • Elsewhere: search for your country's domestic violence helpline, or tell a pastor or a friend you trust.

Would you like us to pray for you? Send your prayer request through the Lifting The Fog Foundation website, or write to info@liftingthefog.ca. You don't need to give your full names.

This page is general encouragement and is not medical, legal or professional counselling advice.
